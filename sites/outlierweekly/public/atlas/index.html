The Town Changed Its Name. The Map Should Not Guess.

Cemini23 · 26 October 2026

Atto shipped a free historical atlas of Italian towns on 25 October 2026. It covers 9,869 places from 1796 to 2026. You type a town and a year, and it names the sovereign regime, the historical province, the modern comune, and the State Archive that holds the records. The letter explains the one rule behind it: the map does not invent a coordinate.

What does the atlas tell you?

The 26 October 2026 issue The Town Changed Its Name. The Map Should Not Guess. is the canonical write-up. It describes a lookup with four answers for one town and one year: the sovereign regime on that ground, the historical province that held jurisdiction, the modern comune, and the State Archive or Diocesan Archive that keeps the civil records. The date inside a year is 1 July. The map colors the modern provincial mesh by the regime on that ground for the selected year. The lines are modern borders, not a historical survey. The page is free, needs no account, and needs no API key.

What are the five free tools?

Why can it not invent a coordinate?

A record may say a person was born in a village in the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies in 1845. That village was a comune then. It may be a frazione or a suppressed comune today. The province changed. The regime changed twice. A language model asked to place that village will often emit a latitude and a longitude, and the number will be invented. A wrong pin looks authoritative, so the reader cannot tell the guess from the fact. The atlas removes coordinates from the agent's job. An agent or a reader passes only what the record says, in a two-parameter URL, ?year=1884&place=Castrogiovanni. The client owns the gazetteer, the administrative span history, and the boundary geometry. The same resolver runs in the command line and in the browser, so the two cannot disagree.

Which historical rules does it encode?

  • The 1871 Veneto gap. Veneto and Mantua joined the Kingdom of Italy in 1866, but unified civil registration did not take effect there until 1 September 1871. A query for 1868 routes to parish registers.
  • The island exemptions. Sicily begins civil registration on 1 January 1820, under the Bourbon system. Sardinia begins in 1838, under the Savoy system, and priests often kept the register.
  • Paper is not gone. The modern digital registry covers current acts. Acts from about 1809 through the 1990s stay in paper registers at the comune and the provincial State Archive.
  • No fake surname hotspot. The surname tool separates curated regional roots, toponymic matches, and foundling surnames such as Esposito, Innocenti, Proietti, and Colombo. Where a surname has no single epicenter, the page says so.

Where does the free atlas stop and the paid kit start?

The atlas is public. It answers questions about places, borders, and archives. No family data goes in, so a public lookup cannot leak. Atto is the private side: the local desktop kit that holds the family tree, the scans, the discrepancy checks, and the citizenship packet on your own Mac or Windows machine, with every lasting change gated on your approval. The atlas tells you which archive holds the book. Atto holds the evidence. The kit is a one-time $299. Outlier Weekly does not sell it and does not take the payment. Is this citizenship or legal advice?

  • Not legal advice. The atlas names archives and jurisdictions. It does not tell you whether you qualify for citizenship.
  • Not a filing service. You still request the acts and you still file, or you hire counsel.
  • Not a records archive. The atlas points at the office that holds a record. It does not hold the record.
  • Not a census. The surname tool is a linguistic estimate, and it marks the cases where it cannot place a name.
