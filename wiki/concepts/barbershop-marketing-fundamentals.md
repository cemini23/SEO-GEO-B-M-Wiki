## Raw Concept

Concept hub for the **industry-specific patterns** of barbershop marketing — what makes the barbershop vertical different from generic local-business marketing. Sources will be ingested progressively. This page frames the SHAPE of the industry's marketing dynamics; quantitative claims need 2026-current verification.

## Narrative

Barbershops have a few industry-specific characteristics that shape every marketing decision:

### High-frequency, high-LTV, taste-bound

- **Visit frequency**: typical men's haircut cadence is **2-6 weeks** (fades faster, longer styles slower). `[NEEDS VERIFICATION 2026-05-07]` for current local-market average — varies by region and demographic.
- **Customer LTV**: a customer who visits every 4 weeks at $35/cut for 2 years = $910 lifetime. Acquisition cost of even $50-100 pays back fast.
- **Taste-bound**: customers don't switch barbers casually — once a customer finds someone who cuts their hair the way they want, switching costs are high (verbal description + risk of bad cut). Both protective for the operator (low churn) and a barrier (acquisition is harder; customers loyal to a competitor are sticky).

Marketing implications:
- Acquisition matters most; retention is largely a function of cut quality + consistency
- Word-of-mouth and visual portfolio (Instagram) are disproportionately valuable because they overcome the "will this barber match my taste" friction
- Volume-discounting is rare; *quality-bundling* (haircut + beard + hot towel) is the upsell pattern

### Local-only, no e-commerce mix

Unlike many local businesses with adjacent online revenue (restaurant delivery, retail e-commerce, services with online consultations), barbershops are **almost entirely physical-visit-driven**. Adjacent revenue streams (retail products: pomades, oils, branded merch) exist but rarely move the needle. This means:
- Local SEO + GBP + reviews + Instagram are the core. Other channels are secondary.
- "Booking" is the primary conversion event; everything else (foot-traffic, walk-ins, calls) feeds the same funnel.

### Two-shop operator dynamics

(Specific to this wiki's primary user.)

A two-shop operator in the same city faces decisions that single-shop operators don't:

- **Brand consolidation vs per-shop differentiation**: shared brand and marketing (one IG, one website with two locations) vs distinct brands per shop. Most multi-shop operators consolidate; differentiation only makes sense if the two shops target meaningfully different demographics.
- **Service-area cannibalization**: if the two shops are <5 miles apart, they compete in the same local pack for "barbershop near me" queries. This is acceptable (better that two of the operator's own shops own the top-3 than competitors do), but means each shop's individual ranking is harder to push above the other.
- **Cross-shop loyalty**: a customer of shop A might naturally become a customer of shop B if they move/work nearer. Cross-shop scheduling (same booking system, transferable loyalty) reduces leakage.
- **Marketing asset reuse**: photos, IG content, reviews can be partially shared but per-location detail (each shop's interior, each team) must remain distinct on per-location pages and per-GBP listings — Google's duplicate-content detection penalizes copied location pages.

### Local market context

Specific market characteristics affect strategy. See @entities/markets/local-market-template.md (operator fills in):
- Population, demographics, age skew
- Barbershop competitor density (varies wildly by region — dense urban / suburban metros support more shops per square mile than smaller towns)
- Adjacent neighborhoods to target (cities or unincorporated areas within a ~5-15-minute drive)
- Seasonal patterns (snowbird / college / tourist markets have predictable seasonality; many markets are year-round-stable)
- Cultural / language considerations (in markets with significant non-English-speaking communities, bilingual signage / IG content / reviews can be a real differentiator)

### The marketing stack for a typical operator

In rough priority:

1. **GBP (per shop)** — claimed, verified, fully optimized, regularly posted on (see @concepts/google-business-profile.md)
2. **Reviews (per shop)** — active acquisition + responses (see @concepts/reviews-reputation-management.md)
3. **Instagram** — primary social, portfolio + new-customer discovery (see @concepts/social-media-for-barbershops.md)
4. **Website with per-location pages** — the shared funnel that converts above-funnel awareness to bookings (see @concepts/website-essentials-local-business.md)
5. **Booking system** — Square Appointments, Booksy, Vagaro, Squire, or platform-native scheduling. The OS of the customer relationship.
6. **TikTok / Facebook** — supplementary social
7. **Local PR / community** — small-town newspaper feature, neighborhood charity event sponsorship — disproportionately useful for both ranking and brand
8. **Paid ads** — Google Local Services Ads, Meta ads. Generally lower priority than organic for a barbershop in a competitive but not extreme market; revisit only after organic is well-tuned.

### What this wiki can help with vs not

This wiki helps with:
- Drafting review responses
- Drafting Instagram captions
- Drafting website copy + per-location-page content
- Drafting GBP descriptions + Posts
- Drafting FAQ content
- Researching competitors + local search trends
- Auditing schema markup
- Evaluating SEO/social tools

This wiki does NOT help with:
- The actual cutting (the only thing that matters in the long run)
- Hiring + retaining barbers
- Booking-system operational decisions
- Permits, licensing, leases, real-estate
- Anything where the operator's local knowledge is better than any wiki content
